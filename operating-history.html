Engagement narratives

How each engagement actually ran.

Each narrative follows the same arc: the operating problem leadership faced, the structure I put in place, and what changed. Engagements are named at the employer level and figures match my resume materials. Customer, partner, supplier, site, and internal-system details stay out by design.

Microsoft · 2011–2015

Microsoft: governing the Software Assurance Planning Services platform

The operating problem. Microsoft's Software Assurance planning-service programs had grown from separate product pilots into a global, partner-delivered platform carrying roughly $18.5B in enterprise contract exposure and $700M–$1B in outstanding voucher liability. Each program ran its own portal, partner registry, redemption rules, and reimbursement flow. The liability had outgrown the governance.

What I did. Through a multi-year managed-services engagement, I helped consolidate the programs into one global portal and partner registry, standardized partner qualification, deliverable standards, and reimbursement controls, introduced anomaly thresholds and targeted audits for misuse, and owned the executive reporting cadence covering utilization, partner performance, satisfaction, renewal impact, and financial exposure — directing a twelve-person vendor team with 40+ matrixed contributors.

What changed. Annual planning engagements scaled from ~6,000 to ~37,500 while content spend fell from ~$13M to ~$7M; ~$13M in improper reimbursements was recovered in the first year of formal controls; and finance-validated analysis showed an 8% renewal improvement across $1.8B in influenced Software Assurance renewal revenue.

Supports: partner ecosystem governance and external delivery patterns.

T-Mobile · 2021–2024

T-Mobile: resetting portfolio signal across Revenue Technology

The operating problem. T-Mobile's Revenue Technology organization tracked ~500 initiatives across revenue systems and business process programs, with 75+ inconsistent tracking fields and no standardized lifecycle. A material portion of work labeled "active" was unfunded, under-defined, or stalled; distorted work-in-progress hid real capacity, and average delivery cycles had stretched to ~340 days. Execution capability was not the issue — readiness discipline was.

What I did. As an embedded consultant reporting to a Senior Director responsible for 100+ developers, product managers, and testers, I reclassified stalled and under-defined work, normalized the tracking fields into one lifecycle structure, introduced staged readiness gates separating qualification from execution, and shifted executive reporting to change-based discussion of risk and sequencing. Generative AI was applied as a governed clarity layer for documentation and meeting follow-through, with human review intact.

What changed. Average delivery cycle time fell 65%, from ~340 to ~120 days, throughput rose without added headcount, and portfolio conversations moved from status reconciliation to sequencing against real capacity.

Supports: messy demand to executive review and intake and signal modules.

Doosan GridTech · 2024–2026

Doosan GridTech: sequencing software, construction, and security under capital constraint

The operating problem. Doosan GridTech was simultaneously deploying its grid-control platform across three utility-scale battery storage sites — roughly 100MW in a ~$125M capital context — developing the next platform generation, and standing up a cybersecurity program, all drawing on the same constrained engineering capacity. Construction windows were fixed, milestones carried contractual exposure, each deployment stream held 1,000–1,300 tasks, and dependencies between the streams were largely implicit.

What I did. Under CTO and COO sponsorship, I built a single integrated sequencing view, mapped software readiness milestones to construction and commissioning gates, made shared capacity constraints explicit, and ran predictable governance forums focused on changes and risk. AI-assisted exception review focused human attention on dependency gaps and critical-path questions the volume of tasks would otherwise hide.

What changed. Cross-stream conflicts surfaced earlier, capacity contention became visible at portfolio level, and executive sessions shifted from reconstructing status to navigating constraints — without adding formal PMO overhead.

Supports: tradeoffs to executive decision and decision and sequencing modules.

Avalara · 2015–2016

Avalara: forming a PPMO during acquisition-driven hypergrowth

The operating problem. Avalara had grown roughly sixfold in three years through acquisitions and rapid market expansion, carrying 500–700 active and proposed initiatives across engineering, platform services, Salesforce, data, security, and IT. Departments prioritized locally, capital allocation lacked a unified view, and the CIO and CFO needed enterprise visibility without slowing a culture built on autonomy and speed.

What I did. Helped establish a CIO/CFO-sponsored IT governance council and project portfolio management office as a hybrid overlay: centralized visibility and standardized qualification gates while preserving team-level execution autonomy. Required explicit executive sponsorship before initiation, ran bi-weekly cross-department forums, and institutionalized a durable decision log capturing tradeoffs and sequencing rationale.

What changed. Duplicate and low-value work was pruned from the 500–700 initiative portfolio, the company gained its first enterprise portfolio baseline, and capital-allocation conversations became structured and forward-looking ahead of IPO-readiness work.

Supports: executive role fit and PPMO formation proof.

Avalara · 2015–2016

Avalara: stabilizing billing integrity across acquired platforms

The operating problem. Acquisitions had brought Avalara back-end engines that calculated usage charges differently. As customer scale increased, the inconsistency created reconciliation overhead, pricing-communication friction, and audit-exposure risk. A full rewrite risked destabilizing live billing; continued patching compounded fragility.

What I did. Structured a phased transformation of the calculation usage engine under CIO-sponsored governance: reconciled requirements across finance, product, engineering, operations, support, and marketing, sequenced changes to protect billing continuity, enforced validation before each release, and delivered an MVP within roughly nine months.

What changed. Disparate billing methodologies converged on a unified, transparent calculation model, dispute troubleshooting and reimbursement exposure declined, audit posture improved, and the business gained a governable baseline for future pricing evolution.

Supports: delivery readiness to value realization and decision cadence proof.

Microsoft · 2018

Microsoft: closing a GDPR blind spot across acquired entities

The operating problem. As GDPR enforcement began, Microsoft had remediated its core products and centrally governed suppliers — but compliance validation had not yet extended to suppliers supporting acquired entities still operating on legacy procurement workflows. The scope: 362 acquired entities, 9,620 suppliers, and 3,424 suppliers providing GDPR-relevant services, against penalties of up to €20M or 4% of global revenue per violation.

What I did. Through my consulting practice, I served as primary remediation lead under Finance M&A sponsorship: mapped the acquisition-linked supplier landscape, segmented in-scope services, designed compliant process pathways aligned to Microsoft standards, secured formal supplier attestations, coordinated transition of non-compliant vendors, and embedded monitoring safeguards into future acquisition workflows.

What changed. The blind spot closed in nine months without disrupting live supplier operations, and supplier compliance moved from implicit assumption to a structured, repeatable M&A governance control.

Supports: control exposure and readiness review and partner/supplier governance patterns.

Microsoft · 2020–2021

Microsoft Commerce Finance: compressing finance-critical validation cycles

The operating problem. Microsoft Commerce Finance governs systems affecting billing accuracy, revenue recognition, partner settlements, and audit posture. User-acceptance cycles routinely ran 6–9 months: finance experts were constrained by monthly close calendars, readiness criteria were undefined, and senior leaders were repeatedly pulled into late-stage interpretation of incomplete solutions.

What I did. As a Principal Consultant embedded in an eleven-person deployment readiness team, I institutionalized a mandatory pre-UAT readiness phase one to two months ahead of execution: approved workback schedules aligned to finance calendars, explicit entry criteria, test cases defined before validation began, standardized readiness artifacts reused across initiatives, and a shared knowledge repository — across 3–4 concurrent delivery teams of 5–40 contributors each.

What changed. UAT cycles compressed from 6–9 months to 30–45 days, deployment speed improved by roughly 35%, developer change requests fell ~15% as requirements stabilized earlier, and finance-critical release windows became forecastable.

Supports: delivery readiness to value realization and readiness modules.

Microsoft · 2008

Microsoft: governing partner-led pilots for Mobile Device Manager 2008

The operating problem. Microsoft was maturing System Center Mobile Device Manager 2008 through live enterprise deployments rather than lab validation alone: 30 customer environments, 90 concurrent pilots, three iterative deployment cycles, and dependencies spanning product development, field sales, marketing, mobile operators, device makers, and integration partners. The activity existed; a unifying operating layer did not.

What I did. Established a lightweight PMO and operating office over the full pilot motion: the planning handbook and execution framework, portal and software-access administration, partner-service coordination, customer feedback intake, reporting cadence, and direction of a five-person vendor team handling engagement logistics.

What changed. Software, devices, services, communications, and feedback ran through one repeatable rhythm; defect and capability signal flowed reliably into successive builds; and leadership gained a live control surface instead of reconstructing status from scattered tools.

Supports: partner ecosystem governance and launch readiness review.

Microsoft · 2010

Microsoft: converting partner readiness into SharePoint 2010 launch evidence

The operating problem. Ahead of the Office 2010 and SharePoint 2010 launch, Microsoft had broad partner participation across its technical readiness programs but no mechanism for converting that activity into validated, market-facing evidence — and a fixed Worldwide Partner Conference deadline by which proof had to ship.

What I did. Helped stand up the Partner Evidence Program as a structured layer over the existing readiness motions: a formal framework defining qualifying evidence and approval paths, scoped statements of work bringing dedicated delivery capacity into the program, a standardized interview-to-asset content workflow, a 76-page program handbook, and a communications layer reaching both the internal field organization and the partner community.

What changed. Of 727 invited partner organizations, 519 became signed, activated participants; 121 partner-approved solution summaries, 121 approved quotes, and 12 partner videos shipped before the deadline alongside a finished launch bill of materials — giving Microsoft a practical way to distinguish launch-ready partners from interested ones.

Supports: partner ecosystem governance and launch readiness review.

MISO Energy · consulting engagement

MISO Energy: structuring R&D demand for executive comparison

The operating problem. MISO Energy's R&D function received project ideas organically from engineers and departmental sponsors, with no consolidated inventory and no consistent way to compare initiatives. Funding decisions risked being shaped by advocacy and urgency rather than evaluation.

What I did. Through my consulting practice, in partnership with R&D management, I interviewed ~30 engineers and analysts plus five executive stakeholders, consolidated demand into a single portfolio view, established standardized intake criteria and multi-criteria evaluation spanning strategic alignment, technical impact, and risk, and facilitated calibration workshops so scoring meant the same thing across groups.

What changed. Leadership gained its first consolidated, comparable view of R&D demand, executive discussion shifted from advocacy to structured tradeoffs, and the organization kept a repeatable model for future prioritization cycles.

Supports: intake discipline and tradeoff review.

Proof taxonomy

How to read the portfolio without mixing proof types.

Experience evidence

Actual experience summarized at employer level with resume-stated figures. These entries prove scale, stakeholder altitude, operating problem, and capability, not every private detail.

Generalized operating patterns

Reusable frameworks and artifact pages derived from practice but scrubbed of employer names, internal systems, exact dates, and proprietary procedures.

Synthetic public examples

Fictional scenarios and sample tables used to make the module behavior inspectable without implying those examples are client records.

Productized POC modules

Public repositories that show workflow architecture, governance prompts, handoffs, and review boundaries. They do not claim production SaaS or software engineering ownership.

Private or interview-only evidence

Exact figures, source caveats, sensitive mechanics, and deeper contribution context belong in live conversation or private job materials where they can be explained responsibly.

Do-not-use details

Customer names, supplier names, partner names, deployment sites, internal systems, sensitive recovery details, and security-sensitive mechanics stay out of public copy.
