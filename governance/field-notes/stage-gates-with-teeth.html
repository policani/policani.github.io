Stage gates work only when they can kill projects

TL;DR

A gate earns its place only when evidence can change the next commitment of money, people, or scope.

What the paper develops

Ask a portfolio board when its last review changed a project's money, people, or scope. If no one can name a case, the board may be tracking work rather than governing it. A real stage gate is a decision point. It decides whether the organization should continue, redirect, narrow, pause, or stop its next commitment to a project.

This matters because momentum gets harder to break. Leaders announce the work. Teams defend it. Contracts are signed. Early doubt becomes sunk cost, and stopping starts to feel like failure. Research on escalation of commitment shows that personal responsibility and negative framing can keep people tied to a failing course. The average sunk-cost effect is positive, though it varies across studies.

Five properties give a gate teeth

First, the forum needs written authority to act. Its charter should say what it may decide, what still belongs to a sponsor or executive committee, and how conflicts move upward. A condition is real only if funding, staffing, scope, or reliance changes when the forum sets it.

Second, the evidence should fit the next commitment. A short, reversible test needs less proof than a large spend or a dependency that other programs will build around. Start with the decision, then name the few facts that must be true. Put those facts on a one-page evidence map with the test, result, owner, and open question.

Third, all five outcomes must work in practice: continue, redirect, narrow, pause, and stop. Define what happens to people, money, and unfinished work after each choice. Otherwise, every choice except continue becomes a new negotiation, and the board will keep weak work moving because that path is easiest.

Fourth, advocates should not be the only owners of key evidence. A sponsor may argue for the project. Finance can still check the benefit math, operations can test whether the change can be absorbed, and architecture can review the integration claim. This is not a claim of bad faith. It is a way to keep hope from becoming the only forecast.

Fifth, approval conditions need a trigger and an end date. If a key supplier slips, adoption falls, or the cost rises past an agreed point, the decision returns to the forum. An old approval should not become permanent permission for work that has changed.

Test one gate before changing the whole process

Choose one mid-sized decision with real stakes. Replace the standing checklist with a one-page evidence map. Write down the five possible outcomes before the meeting. Record the choice, conditions, dissent, owner, and expiry date in the room. Then confirm that money and people moved as directed.

After two or three reviews, ask a blunt question: What changed because this gate met? If the answer is still nothing material, fix the authority before adding more templates. A gate earns its place when evidence can change the next commitment while the organization still has options.

What to do next

Test one mid-sized gate. Give the forum five real choices, tie evidence to the next commitment, and confirm that money and people move with the decision.

Sources and notes

  1. Stage-Gate International, "Gate Governance." — Stage-Gate International describes gate governance as an established commercial method; it is a vendor source, not outcome evidence.
  2. Bent Flyvbjerg, "Quality Control and Due Diligence in Project Management," 2013 — Flyvbjerg explains the planning fallacy and the use of an outside view in project due diligence.
  3. Robert G. Cooper and Scott J. Edgett, "Portfolio Management: Fundamental for New Product Success," Stage-Gate International — Cooper and Edgett describe portfolio management as a dynamic process that can accelerate, stop, or lower the priority of projects and move resources among them.
  4. Michael Bloch, Sven Blumberg, and Jurgen Laartz, "Delivering large-scale IT projects on time, on budget, and on value," McKinsey & Company, 2012 — The McKinsey-Oxford study reports that large IT projects delivered 56 percent less value than forecast on average; it does not test this paper's gate design.
  5. Marcia Blenko, Michael Mankins, and Paul Rogers, "The Five Steps to Better Decisions," Bain & Company, August 6, 2013 — Bain reports a strong link between decision effectiveness and financial performance across more than 1,000 companies; the evidence is correlational.
  6. Stephen Hall, Dan Lovallo, and Reinier Musters, "How to Put Your Money Where Your Strategy Is," McKinsey & Company, March 1, 2012 — McKinsey reports an association between larger capital shifts across business units and higher returns; it does not show that project stops caused those returns.
  7. Dustin J. Sleesman, Donald E. Conlon, Gerry McNamara, and Jonathan E. Miles, "Cleaning Up the Big Muddy: A Meta-Analytic Review of the Determinants of Escalation of Commitment," Academy of Management Journal 55(3), 2012, 541-562 — This meta-analysis links personal responsibility and negative framing with escalation of commitment; it finds a positive average sunk-cost effect that varies widely.
  8. Robert S. Pindyck, “Irreversibility, Uncertainty, and Investment,” Journal of Economic Literature 29(3), 1991, 1110–1148; NBER Working Paper 3307, 1990 — Pindyck's investment theory explains that an irreversible choice gives up the option to wait for more information; it is not a study of project reviews.
