Capital reallocation is the real portfolio decision

TL;DR

In a study of 1,616 companies, the capital each business unit received in one year closely tracked the year before. A short review run by one owner, with a written trigger and common evidence, can end in a move or a recorded hold.

What the paper develops

In portfolios I have governed under a fixed funding ceiling, leaders tended to treat the annual plan as the decision. The effort went into dividing money and people across initiatives, and the split was then defended until the next planning round. That is an observation from practice, not a measured finding.

The decision that tests whether a portfolio is governed comes later. New evidence shows that one initiative is working, another is not, and a third now matters more. Someone must decide whether to move scarce money and people or leave them where last year's case put them. This paper calls that capital reallocation. It argues that a portfolio unable to make this decision between budget cycles is carrying out last year's plan, not governing. It does not claim that this decision matters more than choosing what to fund.

Quarterly reviews are not new. PMI's portfolio standard and Cooper and Edgett's Stage-Gate guidance both expect periodic reviews that can reallocate resources. Neither gives rules for what such a review can decide. In McKinsey's 2026 survey of more than 10,000 leaders and managers, 47 percent said their organizations review budget and talent only once a year or less. Only 30 percent said their organizations reallocate resources across the whole organization. The answers are self-reported and describe whole organizations, not single portfolios.

Stephen Hall, Dan Lovallo, and Reinier Musters studied 1,616 US-listed companies from 1990 to 2005. The capital spending each business unit received correlated 0.92, on average, with what it received the year before. Companies in the top third for moving capital averaged annual shareholder returns about 30 percent higher than those in the bottom third. That is an association. Over periods shorter than three years, the companies that moved more earned lower returns. The authors suggest a lag before results become visible.

The study covers business units, not initiatives. Applying it to a portfolio's initiatives is this paper's inference. The figures show that stickiness is normal and that the payoff from moving may take years to show. They do not promise a return.

Survey respondents named resistance and managerial protectionism, slow decision-making, and unwillingness to make bold calls. The study's authors add anchoring on last year's allocation and politics. This paper adds two causes from its own reasoning. Money already spent may be treated as a reason to spend more. Business cases written to win approval may leave a later review little to test. Some staying put is sound, and moving people has a switching cost that is often not written down.

The fix is a standing review that decides whether a contested resource should move. Four controls make it work. A trigger is published in advance. Every initiative brings the same small evidence set. One owner can move money and people across initiatives. Each review ends in a move or a written reason to hold. What these controls change is what the review can decide.

Two settings keep the review from becoming churn. Each initiative gets a decision horizon, the earliest date its evidence can fairly be judged. A move must also hold across two reviews and be worth more than the cost of moving, including the ramp-up time of the people who move. The paper states these as its own reasoning, since no study sets the right horizon.

A single owner concentrates power. The owner should not sponsor a competing initiative, and the record of each move or hold stays open to the people affected. Some money and people are locked by contract, law, or commitments already made, and the charter lists them. The review costs time, so it starts with one contested resource and replaces a status meeting instead of adding one.

A one-page charter holds the rules: what can move and how often the review meets, the trigger, the evidence, the owner, the move-or-hold record, and the decision horizon. Choose one scarce resource shared by two initiatives, write the charter for it, and run one review before the next planning cycle.

The annual plan tells leaders what they believed at the start. Reallocation shows what they do when the evidence changes.

Sources and notes

  1. Project Management Institute, The Standard for Portfolio Management, Third Edition, 2013, sections 1.8 and 3.1.1, pp. 14 and 31 — PMI's 2013 portfolio standard lists continuous reallocation among a portfolio manager's duties, says reallocating resources faces significant organizational constraints, and allows budget and plan updates on a quarterly or unscheduled basis; it is a professional standard, not an outcome study.
  2. Robert G. Cooper and Scott J. Edgett, Portfolio Management: Fundamental for New Product Success, Stage-Gate International — Cooper and Edgett define portfolio management as a dynamic decision process in which active projects may be sped up, stopped, or deprioritized and resources reallocated, and describe all-project portfolio reviews about four times a year for fast-paced firms; it is a practitioner account.
  3. Alexis Krivkovich, Damian Klingler, Dana Maor, Patrick Guggenberger, and Michael Anzenhofer, The State of Organizations 2026: Three Tectonic Forces That Are Reshaping Organizations, McKinsey & Company, February 19, 2026 — McKinsey reports review cadence, enterprise-wide reallocation, and obstacles named by survey respondents, and says reallocation helps organizations keep up with faster industry cycles; the figures are self-reported and the paper does not treat them as causal proof.
  4. Stephen Hall, Dan Lovallo, and Reinier Musters, How to Put Your Money Where Your Strategy Is, McKinsey & Company, March 1, 2012 — McKinsey's study of 1,616 US-listed companies measures capital received by business units: year-to-year correlation of 0.92, higher returns for top reallocators as an association, and lower returns for heavy reallocators over periods under three years, which the authors suggest is a lag. It does not test initiatives.
  5. U.S. General Accounting Office (now Government Accountability Office), Information Technology Investment Management: A Framework for Assessing and Improving Process Maturity, GAO-04-394G, March 2004 — GAO's 2004 framework for federal technology investments describes portfolio reviews at agreed intervals that compare actual with expected results against predetermined thresholds and adjust resources.
  6. Bent Flyvbjerg, Quality Control and Due Diligence in Project Management: Getting Decisions Right by Taking the Outside View, 2013 — Flyvbjerg reports that front-end cost and benefit estimates typically differ significantly from actual results and proposes checking them against similar completed projects; his evidence comes mostly from large infrastructure projects.
  7. Association for Project Management, What is portfolio management?, APM Body of Knowledge, 8th edition — APM defines portfolio management around strategy and capacity and notes that project sponsors may be required to sacrifice their project priorities for the wider portfolio.
