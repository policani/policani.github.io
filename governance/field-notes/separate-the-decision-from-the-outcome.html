Separate the decision from the outcome

Grade the decision from its timestamp, score the result separately, then update the system the gap points to.

TL;DR

Grade the decision from its timestamp, score the result separately, then update the system the gap points to.

What the paper develops

Why results mislead the review

A program that lands its benefits case is often treated as proof of good judgment. A program that misses is treated as proof of bad judgment. Under uncertainty, neither inference holds. Results still matter, but they need their own grade.

Consider two programs at the same quarterly portfolio review. One consolidated four regional systems and closed early against its benefits case. The other rebuilt a customer identity layer, ran long, and delivered only a fraction of the expected benefit. The committee quickly backed the first sponsor and raised the bar for the second.

The first program benefited when an external regulatory change was deferred. The second lost its schedule when a supplier was acquired and froze its integration roadmap. Neither event was controlled by the sponsor. Yet the review examined the results and inferred the quality of the decisions backward from them.

Grade two records, not one story

A portfolio decision is a commitment made at a specific time, from a specific information set, choosing among stated alternatives and trade-offs, by a named authority. Its quality can be inspected without pretending that good process guarantees success.

At approval, preserve a short record: the frame, alternatives, assumptions, reference class, estimate ranges, accepted risks, and what evidence would make the decision wrong. At review, reconstruct only what was knowable at that timestamp. Keep later knowledge in a separate column.

Then grade the decision inputs and reasoning, score the realized outcome on its own scale, and attribute the gap. A sound decision with a bad outcome calls for a risk or model update, not automatic punishment. A weak decision with a good outcome calls for a method audit before luck becomes precedent.

The four combinations matter because governance usually handles only the obvious two. Sound/good work gets celebrated; weak/bad work gets investigated. The difficult cases carry more learning. Sound/bad tests whether leaders will protect disciplined risk-taking. Weak/good tests whether they can question a success before its tactics become mandatory. Sampling successful programs for the same depth of review is the only direct way to expose that second error.

Attribute the gap before assigning credit

Start with the declared range. A result inside a well-calibrated range is uncertainty resolving. Next compare the approved plan with what was executed: changed scope, missed controls, or ignored dependencies belong to delivery governance. Finally examine comparable decisions. Repeated misses in the same direction are evidence that the estimating model or gate criteria need correction.

These categories overlap, so the review should record the attribution and the evidence behind it rather than force false certainty. The outcome score never edits the decision-quality grade. Both travel together to the committee.

One result should not settle a sponsor's reputation or an estimating model. Calibration accumulates across comparable decisions. Scattered errors around the estimate may indicate an imprecise but roughly centered process; repeated misses in the same direction are a reason to investigate systematic optimism, omitted dependencies, or benefit definitions that do not survive delivery.

Change the incentive inside the review

Review successful programs as well as failures. Report calibration across comparable decisions, not sponsor rankings from isolated outcomes. Protect a sponsor whose documented decision was sound and penalize any attempt to retrofit the record after results arrive.

The practical shift is small: add a decision-quality grade beside every outcome score, preserve the approval-time record, and close every material variance with a named update to the model, execution system, or accountability record. Keep the two grades visible in the same committee packet so neither can quietly displace the other. That separation turns retrospective review from a verdict into an operating-control loop.

The operating move

Put a decision-quality grade beside every outcome score, preserve the approval-time record, and close each material variance with a named update to the model, execution system, or accountability record.

Sources and notes

  1. Jonathan Baron and John C. Hershey, "Outcome Bias in Decision Evaluation," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 54(4), 1988 — the canonical experiment showing that outcomes alter ratings of otherwise identical decisions.
  2. Sriraj Aiyer and colleagues, "Outcomes Affect Evaluations of Decision Quality," International Review of Social Psychology 36(1), 2023 — an open-access direct replication with 692 participants.
  3. Bent Flyvbjerg, "Quality Control and Due Diligence in Project Management," 2013 — the outside view and reference-class discipline for testing what was knowable at approval.
  4. Jan C. van Ours, "Outcome Bias in Managerial Decisions," Journal of Economic Psychology 112, 2026 — open-access field evidence from managerial replacement decisions.
  5. Society of Decision Professionals, "Decision Education at GM" — six elements used here as an inspection framework, not a guarantee of results.
  6. Michael J. Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "Outcome Bias and the Interpreter," Credit Suisse Global Financial Strategies, 2013 — practitioner framing for separating process, skill, and luck.
