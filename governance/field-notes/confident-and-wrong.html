AI operating governance · Field note

Confident and wrong

TL;DR

A fluent AI answer can pass a quick read while carrying a mistake nobody checks, and "have a human review it" does not say who looks, at what, or with the power to say no. What does review need to catch it?

What the paper develops

Fluent AI output can enter work that matters carrying more confidence than evidence, and "have a human review it" often does not catch the problem. This paper takes a position: output that could change a decision or an action that matters should pass a five-part challenge protocol before anyone depends on it.

I have reviewed generated analysis that looked excellent: clean structure, plausible figures, and cited sources. One number felt slightly convenient, so I opened the cited report. The report existed. The number did not. The model had blended two adjacent statistics into one that said what the narrative needed, and nothing in the document gave that away. The only signal was that it agreed with us a little too well.

The hardest AI error to catch is not nonsense; nonsense filters itself. It is the answer that is coherent, specific, and shaped to what the reader expected, and wrong in a way that survives a quick read. The paper's working claim is that an answer that confirms what someone already thinks gets less scrutiny than one that argues back. That claim is an inference. It builds on the long-studied tendency called confirmation bias and on Anthropic research in which people's preference ratings favored answers that matched a user's views. No study cited here measures how closely people check an AI answer at work.

The failure itself is documented. A 2024 study asked four models from 2023 about random federal court cases. ChatGPT 4 gave answers inconsistent with legal fact on 58 percent of the questions and Llama 2 on 88 percent. Those models have been replaced, so the study shows the rate can be high where the stakes are real, not what it is today. In an unreviewed experiment with 257 medical students, AI explanations raised accuracy by 6.3 percentage points when the AI was right (73 percent of cases) and lowered it by 4.9 when it was wrong (27 percent). They helped overall and hurt exactly when the AI erred.

The parts mostly depend on each other in order. The first decides whether the rest apply and how hard. The second feeds the third, because what the attempt to break the answer turns up is the first list of claims to check. The fifth learns from what the others recorded, so it comes last. The one exception is the reviewer for high-stakes output, who is named right after the first part because that person runs the second and third.

Name the decision it could change

A one-minute use declaration before review, which is a planning estimate: which decision, affecting whom, how reversible, at what cost if it is wrong, what the requester expected the answer to say, and for what use, whether brainstorming, drafting, a recommendation, or the basis for an action. Low-stakes uses stop here. Scrutiny concentrates where the stakes do.

Try to break the answer

Before strengthening the answer, try to break it. What would make it false? What other explanation fits the same facts? Who would dispute it, and why? Break hardest where the answer matches what the requester expected. A conclusion that has only survived attempts to improve it has earned nothing yet.

Check the claims outside the answer

Follow the claims the decision rests on to ground outside the answer. Open the cited report. Re-run the calculation. Read the clause itself. Asking the model whether it is sure only consults the same witness again; everything inside the answer is testimony from that one witness.

Name a reviewer, and a route past them

Review works when a named person owns what happens to the output, with relevant expertise, protected time, and the standing to stop the work, and has a defined route for what exceeds them. Volume caps come before generation growth. A reviewer clearing dozens of items a day signs without checking.

Learn from the errors you catch

Every catch is operating evidence. Keep a light failure log, review repeated failures monthly, and give someone authority to change prompts, data, permissions, or review tiers. Otherwise the organization keeps paying for the same lesson in near misses.

Put the five questions in the interface or the work record where people actually depend on the output. Start with one workflow where the stakes are real. The early weeks may turn up catches nobody expected; that is the baseline being found. Then watch one number: how often an outside check changes a claim. It is the share of checked claims that turned out wrong or unsupported, not the workflow's overall error rate, and no source here says how large it should be. If it stays near zero in a tier, test the checkers first with planted known-bad items or a sampled re-check; loosen only if they catch them.

The protocol is the paper's own design, and no study tests it. The white paper answers four objections (distrust, better models, speed, and proof), develops each part with how it typically fails and what counters that, covers the ways a review gets faked (forms filled in by rote, checking the answer against itself, approval in name only), and gives a starter failure log.

What to do next

Before AI output feeds a decision that matters, run five checks. Name the decision it could change. Try to break the answer. Check its claims outside the answer. Name a reviewer who can say no, with a route past them. Learn from the errors caught.

Sources and notes

  1. Raymond S. Nickerson, "Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises," Review of General Psychology 2, no. 2 (1998): 175-220 — A review of research on confirmation bias, the tendency to seek or read evidence in ways that favor beliefs a person already holds. It does not study AI answers.
  2. Mrinank Sharma, Meg Tong, Tomasz Korbak, David Duvenaud, Amanda Askell, Samuel R. Bowman, et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models," arXiv:2310.13548, published as a conference paper at ICLR 2024 — Anthropic researchers found that human preference data favored responses matching a user's views, and that non-expert raters without fact-checking tools sometimes preferred a convincing agreeable answer over a correct one. It does not measure how closely people check AI answers.
  3. OpenAI, "Sycophancy in GPT-4o: What Happened and What We’re Doing About It," April 2025 — OpenAI's account of one April 2025 GPT-4o update that it rolled back as overly flattering or agreeable.
  4. Ziwei Ji, Nayeon Lee, Rita Frieske, et al., "Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation," ACM Computing Surveys, 2023 — A survey of hallucination in language-generation research: output that is fluent and coherent but unfaithful or nonsensical.
  5. Matthew Dahl, Varun Magesh, Mirac Suzgun, and Daniel E. Ho, "Large Legal Fictions: Profiling Legal Hallucinations in Large Language Models," Journal of Legal Analysis 16(1), 2024, 64–93; open version arXiv:2401.01301 — Four 2023 models answered questions about random federal court cases. ChatGPT 4 was inconsistent with legal fact on 58 percent of the questions and Llama 2 on 88 percent. One field, and the models have since been replaced.
  6. Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich H. Manzey, "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration," Human Factors 52, no. 3 (2010): 381–410 — A review of research on automation complacency and automation bias, which appear in experts as well as novices; training or instructions do not prevent automation bias.
  7. Manshu Khanna, Ziyi Wang, Lijia Wei, and Lian Xue, "When Medical AI Explanations Help and When They Harm," arXiv:2512.08424, 2025 — An unreviewed preprint with 257 medical students at one hospital. AI explanations raised accuracy by 6.3 percentage points when the AI was right and lowered it by 4.9 when it was wrong.
  8. Blake Bullwinkel et al., "Lessons From Red Teaming 100 Generative AI Products," Microsoft Research, January 2025 — Lessons from Microsoft's red teaming of more than 100 generative AI products, including that finding failures takes people with expertise and judgment.
