Cutting the budget at approval hides whether the project paid off

TL;DR

Many finance leaders take a project's forecast saving out of next year's budget at approval and close the benefit once the lower budget holds. Where the budget holder fills a shortfall from elsewhere, the budget holds whether or not the project produced the saving, and the benefits check passes. The cut adds false successes but no false misses, so the record leans toward success, and the next similar business case is judged against that record. Keep the cut, but check where the money came from against an operating measure the cut did not set.

What the paper develops

The decision

A project is approved because it will save money. To stop the saving leaking into other spending, the forecast saving comes out of next year's budget at approval. Consultants at Bain call building anticipated savings into the budget at setup the most effective approach [1]. When the lower budget holds through the year, the benefit is closed.

Where the gap goes

After the cut, the risk that the forecast is wrong sits with the budget holder, who has to live within the lower number. People held to a budget try to meet it, by legitimate or illegitimate means, including delaying purchases [2]. Nine months into 2015/16, English NHS providers had planned for 90 percent of their savings to be continuing and got 75 percent; acute hospitals covered 211 million pounds of their shortfall with one-off savings [3]. Part of a held budget may have come from elsewhere.

What the record learns

If the benefit is closed when the same budget that was cut holds, the check cannot tell where the saving came from. Where the gap can be filled, a held budget is recorded both when the project worked and when it did not. The cut adds only false successes, so the record leans toward success. Where past results judge new cases, the next similar business case looks safer than it is. The money is kept; some of the ability to tell good projects from bad ones is spent.

What to check instead

Keep the cut if budget discipline needs it. At approval, name an operating measure the project changes, taken from the system where the work is recorded, with its baseline and what each unit of change actually saves in cash. At year end, ask how the lower budget was met and whether each part will recur, and check the answer against the measure. Count as the project's saving the smaller of the measure's movement priced at what each unit saves and the actual fall in spending on the cost line. This applies to cost savings tracked only in money against the budget that was cut.

What to do next

Keep the cut, but before closing the benefit, ask how the lower budget was met and check the answer against an operating measure the project changes.
