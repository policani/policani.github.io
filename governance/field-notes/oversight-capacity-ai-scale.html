AI operating governance · Field note

AI can grow only as fast as people can supervise it

TL;DR

Every AI agent adds work that a named person must review, own, and explain.

What the paper develops

You are told to add more AI agents, find more uses, and get more work done. The plan may count model cost and technical skill. It may not ask a harder question: How many AI decisions can your people still review, explain, and stand behind?

Every agent adds output. It also adds work for people who check important results, handle exceptions, and answer when the system is wrong. If agents grow faster than that human work, the company gains output it cannot fully own.

McKinsey expects human oversight to limit how widely companies can use AI agents. It also reports that teams of two to five people can already supervise 50 to 100 specialized agents. That is an early figure, not a safe benchmark for every company. It still points to the question leaders need to answer: Where is the limit for this team and this work?

Put a number on supervision

The span of supervision is how many agents or units of AI work one accountable person can genuinely own. The span gets smaller when errors are costly, hard to see, or hard to reverse. It can be larger when errors are easy to spot and fix.

Use a weekly test. Can the named owner explain what the agent did, what was checked, what failed, and what changed? If the honest answer is no, the team has already taken on more than it can supervise.

Review by risk

Oversight does not mean reading every output. Low-risk work may need sampling. High-risk work may need a person to approve each case. New or uncertain work may need tighter review until the team knows its failure patterns. Write down what is sampled, what always gets checked, what triggers a deeper review, and who can stop the workflow.

Use tools, but keep a human owner

Critic agents, guardrails, monitoring, tests, and incident response can help a small team supervise more work. They can find common errors and send unusual cases to people. They still need their own tests and monitoring. A named person must be able to change or stop them.

Treat review time as shared capacity

Every live AI system draws on a shared group of reviewers and experts. Each funding request should name the owner, expected review load, type of review, experts needed for hard cases, and conditions that would pause or stop the work. When that pool is full, move attention away from stable, low-risk work before adding more demand.

Start with one workflow

Track the owner's review and follow-up time for four weeks. Check whether exceptions reached the right person and whether the owner can explain what changed. Use that evidence to adjust the span before another agent goes live. AI can grow only as fast as the organization can still answer for what it does.

What to do next

Measure one workflow for four weeks. Count the owner's review and follow-up time, then adjust the number of live agents before adding another.

Sources and notes

  1. Alexander Sukharevsky, Alexis Krivkovich, Arne Gast, Arsen Storozhev, Dana Maor, Deepak Mahadevan, Lari Hämäläinen, and Sandra Durth, "The agentic organization: Contours of the next paradigm for the AI era," McKinsey & Company, September 26, 2025 — McKinsey expects human oversight to limit wider use of AI agents and reports one early team-to-agent figure; the paper does not treat it as a benchmark.
  2. David Mallon, Brad Kreit, and Natasha Buckley, "Rethinking operating models for humans with agents," Deloitte Insights, April 2, 2026 — Deloitte describes agents watching other agents, with people retaining oversight of the control layer.
  3. Chloe Autio, Reva Schwartz, Jesse Dunietz, Shomik Jain, Martin Stanley, Elham Tabassi, Patrick Hall, and Kamie Roberts, "Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile," NIST AI 600-1, July 26, 2024 — The NIST Generative AI Profile suggests ongoing monitoring, red-team exercises, independent evaluation, incident response, and structured feedback.
  4. National Institute of Standards and Technology, "AI Risk Management Framework Core," AI RMF 1.0, 2023 — The NIST AI RMF Core places governance, mapping, measurement, and risk response in a continuing cycle.
  5. Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich H. Manzey, "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration," Human Factors 52, no. 3 (2010): 381-410 — Parasuraman and Manzey review evidence that competing tasks and consistently reliable automation can weaken human monitoring; the studies do not set an enterprise AI staffing ratio.
  6. M. L. Cummings and P. J. Mitchell, "Operator Scheduling Strategies in Supervisory Control of Multiple UAVs," Aerospace Science and Technology 11 (2007): 339-348 — Cummings and Mitchell report cognitive saturation and automation bias in a simulation with one person supervising several semi-autonomous aircraft.
  7. M. L. Cummings and Stephanie Guerlain, “Developing Operator Capacity Estimates for Supervisory Control of Autonomous Vehicles,” Human Factors 49, no. 1 (2007): 1–15 — Cummings and Guerlain report lower performance and awareness at 16 missiles in one simulation with 42 U.S. Navy personnel; the result does not set a business AI limit.
