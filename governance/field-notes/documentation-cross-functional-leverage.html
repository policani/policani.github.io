The write-up you skipped never gets blamed

TL;DR

When a meeting ends and nobody writes down what was decided, the next team has to stop and ask or start and guess. The hour you saved comes back in their queue, logged as a clarification, a redesign, or a reopened decision. What it never gets logged as is the write-up nobody did.

What the paper develops

Your steering group approved a change. The next team is ready to start. The schedule is already slipping, so someone makes a sensible-sounding offer: begin the handoff now, write up the decision later. It sounds fine, because everyone in the meeting already knows what happened. The next team does not.

They need to know what changed, why it changed, what you agreed to bend, and who is allowed to change it again. Without that, the person picking up the work has two choices: stop and ask, or start and guess. Both put more pressure on the schedule you were trying to protect.

So does starting sooner actually save time, once the decision has to cross into someone else's work? The answer shows up in what happens after the meeting.

Where the time actually goes

Writing it up is easy to skip because the cost is visible and immediate — an hour, right now, while the schedule slips. Starting the handoff is immediate too. The write-up looks like a separate job you can do any time.

Then a product team gets the scope change and comes back asking why. Operations designs the standard path and then finds out an exception was already agreed. A new manager reopens a settled decision because nobody wrote down what would have to change for it to be revisited.

Each of those gets logged as something else — a clarification, a redesign, a reopened decision. The original choice to skip the write-up never appears in the account of the delay. That is why it never gets fixed: nothing in the record points back to it.

Let the next team decide when it is done

Records matter most at the seams — a decision moving from a steering group to delivery, a design moving between functions, a program moving into operations, one owner handing off to the next. This is where knowledge has to move from the people who have it to people who do not, and survive in a form those people can use.

Length and polish prove nothing. Hand the record to the person receiving the work and ask them to trace the action they are supposed to take back to the decision, the reason behind it, where it stops applying, and who can change it. If they have to come back to you for another meeting, that gap is what the handoff is still missing.

A record that passes does four jobs. It lets the next team act. It keeps the decision and the reasoning together. It stays useful after people leave. And it gives a person, or an AI system, something reliable to look up later. All four depend on the record working without its author standing next to it.

AI cannot tell you what the page left out

A retrieval system can only find what the page actually contains. A missing reason, a missing exception, a missing owner, a missing date — none of that can be recovered from a page that never captured it. Research on retrieval systems finds that problems starting at the source carry through to the answer, and NIST lists where data came from and how good it is among the things organizations should write down. Neither tells a retrieval system who keeps the page current. Only the page can do that.

A colleague who hits an incomplete page knows who to call. A retrieval system sees the page, whatever is attached to it, and nothing else. A better model cannot supply context that was never written down — though bad documentation is only one of the things that can make an AI answer wrong.

Give the next team five things

At the next handoff that matters, require a short record with a name on it that answers five questions. What did we decide, and what is it meant to achieve? Why did we pick it? What did we agree to bend or leave open, and what cannot change? What happens next, and who owns it? What date or event should bring this back up?

The person who made the decision stays responsible for the content even if someone else writes the words. They confirm the reasoning, the limits, and when to revisit. The person receiving it confirms they can actually use it.

Ownership does not end at acceptance. A record people rely on turns dangerous when conditions change underneath it, so someone has to be able to fix it, retire it, or flag the part where someone now has to decide for themselves. A policy change, a system change, an exception, a new decision, or a review date is what should trigger that.

Back to the handoff you were about to rush. If the record passes the next team's test, they can start without guessing, and someone is on the hook for keeping it accurate. If it fails, you know exactly what to do — fill in what is missing before you hand it over. The schedule will still be urgent. What changes is when you call it done: not when the meeting ends, but when the person receiving the work can use what you wrote.

What to do next

Before you hand work to another team, write down what you decided, why, what you agreed to bend, who does what next, and when to revisit it. Then let them say whether the handoff is done.
