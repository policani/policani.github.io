AI operating governance · Field note

Your approver will answer for the agent you designed

TL;DR

When you put an AI agent to work, a person approving each consequential action seems to make someone answerable. But the approval step tends to decide who will be found at fault: after a failure the review asks why the approver approved, and the limits you set for the agent get less attention. That holds even for a careful approver, who checks single actions, not your limits. Test each approval step with known-wrong actions mixed into real work, set up so they cannot take effect. Keep it where it catches them, narrow the agent elsewhere, and write down that any failure review starts with the agent's limits.

What the paper develops

The decision

You are putting an AI agent to work in a process. The standard design has a person approve each consequential action, keeps permissions to the minimum, adds a way to stop it and names someone accountable. You expect the approval step to catch the agent's mistakes and to make someone answerable, because a person signed. Most owners already know approvers miss errors: people tend to defer to the system, even after training [1]. The question is the second job.

Where the blame lands

When a system run jointly by people and machines fails in a visible way and is judged quickly, the blame tends to settle on the nearest person, and the technology is kept faultless [2]. An autopilot counts as working as long as it gives the pilot time to take over, so when a failure lies between pilot and machine, the certification finds nothing wrong with the machine. This paper's reasoning is that an agent's approval step has the same shape: a review that asks which part failed to do what the design expected finds the approval the easiest answer. Green argues that requiring human review also makes a system look safe to adopt, which lowers scrutiny of the people who set it up, even when the reviewer is careful [1].

Two kinds of answerability

Holding the approver answerable does help. Pilots who felt accountable for how they used automation checked it more and made fewer errors with it [3], and for high-risk systems the EU AI Act requires that the people given oversight have the competence, training, authority and support to do it [4]. But that is answerability for checking. The approver answers for how well they checked, given time, information and authority. The owner, who decides what the agent may do and can stop it, answers for those limits.

What to change

Test each approval step: mix known-wrong actions into the approver's normal queue, set up so they cannot take effect, give the approver only the time and information they really have, and count how many they stop. Keep the step where it catches them at a rate you accept. Elsewhere, narrow what the agent may do on its own. Where law or contract requires an approval, keep it and equip the approver. In every case, write down before launch that any failure review starts with the agent's limits. The test is this paper's recommendation, not a finding from these sources.

What to do next

Before the agent starts, write down three things. Who decides what the agent may do and can stop it; that person answers for its limits. Which actions need approval, and the test result behind each one. And that any failure review starts with the agent's permissions, rules, data and limits, and reaches the approver's checking only after that.
