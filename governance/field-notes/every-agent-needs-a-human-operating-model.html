AI operating governance · Field note

Every agent needs a human operating model

TL;DR

A capable AI agent clears its pilot, and the decision to switch it on lands with one leader who will answer for everything it does next. What has to be true before it is allowed to touch real work?

What the paper develops

A capable AI agent, software that takes actions on its own, clears its pilot. The decision to put it into production lands on one leader's desk. Approving it means giving the agent a live workflow and letting it act on real customers and real data. At that moment there is no settled answer to a plain question. The agent makes a judgment call, escalates the wrong case, or returns an output that looks finished and is wrong. Who in the organization owns that result?

This paper takes a position: an AI agent should not act on real work until one named person, the owner, can answer seven questions about it. It is a position, not a finding. No source used here compares agents that went live with these answers against agents that went live without them. The position would be wrong for a kind of work if agents with no named owner, no written rules on what they may decide, and no way to halt them made no more costly mistakes than governed ones. This paper expects it to matter least where mistakes are cheap and easy to undo.

Gartner forecasts that more than 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls. That is a prediction, not a count. Gartner names immature models too, but its other reasons point to how agents are chosen and governed.

The canvas is a one-page sheet with one answer for each of seven elements. Part of it is not new: Deloitte's article already lists three elements, the split of decisions, the stop mechanisms, and the activity logging. This paper adds three things: one named owner who answers for all seven, a funding gate (an agent is not funded for real work until its canvas is complete), and the canvas itself as the written record the gate checks. No source tests whether those additions improve outcomes.

1. Role and scope

What is it for, and where does it stop? Scope creep, where duties widen without a decision, is one way an agent ends up making decisions nobody designed it to make.

2. What it may decide, and what it may not

What may it decide alone, what needs a sign-off, and what must it escalate? If the rule is not written, the agent has the widest authority anyone can read into it.

3. How it escalates and stops

A path for cases it should not handle, and a stop switch that halts it and exists before it is needed. One bad assumption at speed can become many bad outputs.

4. What has to challenge it

Before anyone trusts an output, what checked it, and what did that check catch? Confident wrong answers can be the costly ones.

5. What a good result looks like

Define good before the agent produces anything. Otherwise review collapses into a check for plausibility, which a fluent model can pass without being right.

6. What evidence it leaves

A trail that lets a person work out what the agent did and why. Keeping it is how an organization would turn standards like NIST's into daily practice.

7. Who owns the result

One named person who owns the outcomes. Not a committee. Not the vendor. If no name attaches, the agent should not go live.

The owner is the person who owns the business result of the workflow the agent works in, the one whose target or budget takes the loss when it goes wrong. Test any candidate with four questions. Who takes the loss? Who can change the workflow's rules? Who can stop the agent today without asking anyone? Who has the time? The owner's time is a cost that is easy to leave out of a plan, so it goes in the budget. How many agents one owner can answer for is a separate question, covered in "The Limit on AI Agents May Be the People Who Supervise Them."

Make a completed canvas a requirement for funding real work. The owner writes it, a few sentences per answer, and revises it when the agent's scope, model, or vendor changes. A missing answer holds the funding, and the agent can continue only as a limited test whose output a person checks first. A weak answer narrows what the agent is funded to do.

The white paper works through all seven elements, gives three illustrations, answers the objections, and says what the evidence does not show.

What to do next

Before funding an agent, have its owner write the Agent Operating-Model Canvas: one answer for each of seven elements, from what the agent may decide to who owns the result. A missing answer holds the funding for real work. Put the owner's time in the budget.
