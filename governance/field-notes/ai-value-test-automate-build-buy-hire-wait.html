AI operating governance · Field note

Automate, build, buy, hire, or wait

TL;DR

In this paper's observation, an AI request often arrives with its response already chosen: buy this, build that. Gartner reports that at least half of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025. The paper recommends comparing five responses first, with proof set by how hard each is to undo. A wait gets a record of what brings the request back, who watches for it, and when to look again. The rule is the paper's own design, and no source tests it.

What the paper develops

A steering group, the people who decide where a company's money and staff go, receives an AI request that has already picked its response. A team wants to buy a drafting assistant, build an agent, or automate a report, and the review is asked to say yes or no. That is this paper's observation of how requests are often framed. No source counts how often.

Gartner reported in January 2026 that at least half of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear business value. In July 2024 it had forecast 30 percent, citing the same four causes. The article shows no method, so neither figure says how often a different choice would have helped. RAND's interviews with 65 data scientists and engineers also name missing data among five leading causes of failed AI projects.

Once the recurring work and the step that most limits it are named, compare five responses before funding any of them: automate the work, build a capability, buy a tool, hire or train people, or wait. Ask the same six questions of each. Does it relieve the limiting step? Is what it needs in place? What does it cost over the period the organization is committed to it? What can go wrong? Can the organization carry it? How hard is it to undo?

Drop any response that cannot relieve the limiting step. Send one that needs something not yet in place to wait, with the missing item as its trigger. Replace one that is hard to undo and lacks the proof its level calls for with a smaller version that is easy to undo. Rank the rest by how much each relieves against its total cost for each year of commitment. When two are close, the one that is easier to undo wins. The business owner, the leader accountable for the work's results, decides, and a portfolio analyst keeps all five on one page. The rule and the planning estimates of its cost are the paper's own design, and no source tests them.

Pindyck's analysis of investment under uncertainty shows that an irreversible choice gives up the chance to wait for new information, and that the lost chance is a cost. The paper turns that idea into three levels. A response that is easy to undo needs the baseline, a stated reason, and a review date. One that is costly to undo needs a small test in real work. One that is practically permanent, such as a multi-year contract, a custom build, or removed roles, needs the full operating case and a written way out, and the steering group decides.

A wait is a response with its own record: a trigger, an owner, a review date, and a stated cost of delay. A trigger is a named event or measurement that brings the request back for a decision. A wait with no trigger or no owner counts as a stop.

The comparison can add process to every idea, so it scales with how hard a response is to undo. Waiting can drift, so the review date forces a decision. Five ways the choice goes wrong are automation theater, prestige building, procurement substitution, headcount avoidance, and passive waiting, and the paper says which part of the rule catches each. Pindyck's analysis is theory about business investment in general, and no source compares building with buying or hiring.

Before the next steering meeting, take one AI request whose problem has been named. Have a portfolio analyst write the five responses on one page, one line each, answering the same six questions. The business owner marks each response's level, applies the screen, ranks what remains, settles any tie toward the response that is easier to undo, and states the proof the chosen response must show before it expands. If the choice is to wait, the page carries the trigger, the wait's owner, the review date, and what the delay costs.

What to do next

Take one AI request whose problem has been named. Put the five responses on one page, remove any that fail the screen, and set the proof by how hard each is to undo. If the result is to wait, write what brings the request back, who watches for it, the review date, and what the delay costs.

Gartner, "Gartner Predicts 30% of Generative AI Projects Will Be Abandoned After Proof of Concept By End of 2025," July 29, 2024 — Gartner's July 2024 release forecasts that at least 30 percent of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025. It also describes expanding an investment that meets expectations, or exploring alternatives if it falls short. It is a forecast and shows no method.
  2. Arun Chandrasekaran, "Why 50% of GenAI Projects Fail - And How to Beat the Odds," Gartner, January 26, 2026 — Gartner reports that at least half of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, or unclear business value. The page says Gartner analyzed hundreds of implementations and shows no method.
  3. James Ryseff, Brandon F. De Bruhl, and Sydne J. Newberry, "The Root Causes of Failure for Artificial Intelligence Projects and How They Can Succeed: Avoiding the Anti-Patterns of AI," RAND Corporation, 2024 — RAND interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers about failed AI projects and names five root causes, including missing data, and describes leaders demanding AI for problems that simple rules could solve. Most interviewees were engineers, not executives, and the interviews are not a count of projects.
  4. Gartner, "Gartner Predicts Over 40% of Agentic AI Projects Will Be Canceled by End of 2027," June 25, 2025 — Gartner says many vendors relabel existing assistants, chatbots, and automation tools as agentic AI, and estimates that only about 130 of the thousands of agentic AI vendors are real. The estimate covers agentic AI vendors only and shows no method.
  5. Robert S. Pindyck, “Irreversibility, Uncertainty, and Investment,” Journal of Economic Literature 29(3), 1991, 1110–1148; NBER Working Paper 3307, 1990 — Pindyck's economic analysis of investment under uncertainty: an irreversible investment gives up the chance to wait for new information, which is a cost, and delay has its own cost. It is theory about business investment in general, not a study of AI.
  6. NIST, "AI Risk Management Framework Core," excerpt from AI RMF 1.0, 2023 — NIST's framework lists procedures for decommissioning AI systems and for superseding, disengaging, or deactivating one whose results are inconsistent with its intended use. It is voluntary and does not set how much proof a purchase needs.
  7. Johannes-Tobias Lorenz, Joshan Cherian Abraham, Robert Levin, and Douglas Ziman, "From promise to impact: How companies can measure and realize the full value of AI," McKinsey & Company, April 24, 2026 — McKinsey says many organizations deploy AI in ways that are more visible than valuable, and sets out an approach to measuring AI value. It is a consultancy's view, not a measured rate.
