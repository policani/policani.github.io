Governing AI as operational change, not software activity

Most enterprise AI reporting answers a question nobody funded: are people using the tool? The question leadership actually paid for is whether recurring work got better. Those are different questions, and the gap between them is where AI programs fail.

The argument in one line

AI utilization is a workflow-performance question, not a software-activity question — and it should be governed by the same evidence discipline as any other portfolio investment.

Microsoft Copilot, OpenAI Enterprise, and comparable platforms expose seat-level telemetry by default: active users, session frequency, feature engagement, adoption curves. Most organizations can produce these reports within days of deployment, and many treat them as evidence of progress. They are evidence of software activity. Leadership funds AI to change recurring work — faster cycle times, more throughput, less rework, better decisions at the point of action — and none of those outcomes appear in an active-user dashboard.

The cost of that gap is now documented at scale. MIT's NANDA initiative found that 95 percent of enterprise generative-AI pilots deliver no measurable P&L impact, against an estimated $30–40 billion in annual spending. Gartner predicted in 2024 that at least 30 percent of generative-AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing cost and unclear business value as primary reasons. Whatever the exact numbers turn out to be in hindsight, the direction is consistent: activity everywhere, decision-grade evidence almost nowhere.

Why the usual reporting fails

Activity metrics break down for reasons any portfolio operator will recognize. There is no denominator — a user who opened the tool twice looks identical to one whose weekly workflow depends on it. Power users distort averages, making a narrow footprint look like broad deployment. Baselines rarely exist, so post-launch claims are retrospective and anecdotal. And usage data lives in one system while business outcomes live in Jira, ServiceNow, Salesforce, or a BI layer, with no deliberate bridge between them. The result is visible reporting without decision-grade signal.

A standard worth governing against

Meaningful adoption is a condition, not a rate: AI is in the critical path of recurring work, at sufficient scale and consistency, to produce measurable change in workflow outcomes or decision inputs — across more than a small concentration of power users. Meeting that standard takes three layers working together. Platform telemetry confirms breadth: who is active, how often, whether adoption is concentrated. Workflow performance metrics carry the value question: did cycle time improve, did throughput rise, did quality hold, did rework fall — answers that already exist in the systems organizations use to run operations. And explanatory validation supplies interpretation: pre/post comparison on the same workflow, matched cohorts, manager pulse checks, quality sampling. Perfect attribution is not the goal. Decision-grade evidence is. The programs that last tend to build this discipline early — in Gartner's maturity research, high-maturity organizations were far more likely to keep AI in production for years and to report that business units actually trust the tools: 57 percent, against 14 percent at low maturity.

What the model measures in practice

The measurement is deliberately ordinary, because the data usually already exists. In service and case work, the signals are case cycle time, throughput, reopen rate within a set window, and escalation rate — pulled from the systems that already run the operation, with a check that usage is not concentrated in a handful of agents before any team-level claim is made. In document and proposal work, they are time to a usable first draft, revision cycles, and approval cycle time, paired with a manager's read on whether recovered time became volume, quality, or speed. In requirements and internal analysis, they are draft turnaround and revision count paired with at least one downstream-quality measure, so a speed gain is not booked while rework quietly rises. None of this needs new instrumentation. It needs the operating data reviewed alongside the telemetry rather than separately from it, an integration the major platforms now support directly by letting organizations bring their own operational metrics into the analytics layer.

What makes the reading defensible is a bar set before measurement starts: a baseline window and a matched post-enablement window (four to eight weeks each, adjusted to the workflow's cycle), at least two operating metrics, at least one comparison method — pre/post, matched cohort, or quality sampling — and sign-off from the person who owns the workflow and the person who owns the source data. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes the same move in its MEASURE function: use quantitative or qualitative methods to benchmark and monitor, and document what cannot yet be measured reliably. Programs that define the threshold after the fact almost always define it to fit the answer they already have.

Why heavier measurement backfires

The instinct to prove value with more surveillance — keystroke logs, screen capture, per-task attribution — works against the adoption it is meant to track. In controlled studies from Cornell's ILR School, employees under algorithmic monitoring reported a greater loss of autonomy than those overseen by human managers and were more likely to resist, complain, and consider leaving; the effect held across four experiments and reversed only when monitoring was framed as developmental rather than evaluative. Microsoft learned the public version of this in 2020, stripping individual-level metrics from its Productivity Score feature within days of criticism. Heavy measurement architecture does not just create exposure. It damages the thing it is supposed to measure.

The through-line is a claim about accountability, not tooling. If an AI program reports monthly and cannot name the workflow that improved, the baseline it improved against, and the person accountable for that claim, it is reporting software activity. More telemetry will not fix that. Treat AI like every other investment the portfolio governs: funded against an outcome, measured against a baseline, and owned by someone who has to defend the number.
