Compare AI proposals on the work they change, not the demo

TL;DR

A portfolio review can face several AI proposals for the same people and budget, each backed by different evidence. The paper recommends one page for each, answering the same six questions, so the review can compare them side by side.

What the paper develops

You chair the portfolio review that decides which AI proposals receive scarce capacity: the people and budget that cannot cover every request. One team brings a working assistant. Another has a vendor demo. A third shows time saved on drafting and analysis. All three can produce something. None has shown which work should change, what result would matter, or why AI is the right investment.

This paper recommends that the review ask for the same one-page opportunity brief for every proposal and compare the proposals on the briefs, not on the demos. The business owner, the leader accountable for the results of the work the proposal would change, writes the brief. It answers six questions: what work or decision should change, what is happening there now, how value could appear, what evidence would earn the next step, who owns the result and what AI may decide, and what else could solve the problem. The next step is a small funded test with a result stated in advance. The brief does not produce a score. Its first job is to show which proposals can be compared yet, because a proposal with a missing answer cannot be ranked against one without.

A field study of 5,179 customer-support agents at one company found a 14 percent average productivity gain from an AI assistant, with the largest gains for novice workers. It shows that a tool can help one defined workflow. It does not show which work a company should choose to change, or that the workflow deserves capacity ahead of another team's proposal. In McKinsey's 2025 survey, 88 percent of respondents said their organizations regularly use AI in at least one function, and 39 percent attributed any level of earnings impact to AI at the enterprise level; most of those said less than 5 percent. Gartner reports that at least half of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept, and says projects without a clear way to rank use cases or measure success lack measurable business value.

This paper's reading is that a demo, a usage count, and a local time saving answer different questions about a tool or a task, so they do not rank proposals against each other. No source tests that reading.

The portfolio analyst lays the briefs side by side and marks each answer as given or missing. A reviewer outside the proposing teams checks each baseline, the measurement of the problem today, and each evidence test, the result that would justify more investment. The review chair then decides, with no formula, because a score would hide the judgment of weighing delay against errors, cost, and risk, and writes one line of reasoning for each decision. A weak answer counts against a proposal. A missing answer means the proposal cannot be compared yet, so it is held with the missing answers named and receives no scarce capacity until they are supplied. Each proposal ends in one of four outcomes: fund the next step, hold it with the missing answers named, combine it with other work, or stop it.

A brief can slow exploration, so a low-cost trial by one team needs none. A business case may already answer the six questions, and then no second document is needed. A polished page or a hopeful baseline can win, so a reviewer outside the proposing teams checks the baseline. With no formula, the chair's discretion is limited only by the written reason for each decision. The estimates in a brief are guesses, which the brief makes checkable. No source tests the brief.

Before the next portfolio review, ask the business owner of each AI proposal to write the one-page brief, and ask that no brief name a tool before it names the work. The analyst marks the gaps before the meeting. The chair then decides each proposal's outcome and writes one line saying why. Expect missing answers the first time. The list of them is the first result.

What to do next

Before the next review, ask the business owner of each AI proposal for the one-page brief. The analyst marks each answer as given or missing, and the chair decides each proposal's outcome and writes one line saying why.

Sources and notes

  1. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey R. Raymond, "Generative AI at Work," NBER Working Paper 31161, April 2023; revised November 2023; published in The Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(2), 2025, 889-942 — a field study of 5,179 customer-support agents at one company found a 14 percent average productivity gain from an AI assistant, 34 percent for novice and low-skilled workers and minimal for experienced workers. It measures one tool, one workflow, and one company.
  2. Alex Singla, Alexander Sukharevsky, Bryce Hall, Lareina Yee, Michael Chui, and Tara Balakrishnan, "The state of AI in 2025: Agents, innovation, and transformation," McKinsey & Company, November 5, 2025 — McKinsey's survey of 1,993 respondents reports that 88 percent regularly use AI in at least one function and that 39 percent attribute any level of earnings impact to AI at the enterprise level, most of them under 5 percent. These are respondents' own reports, so the survey cannot show cause.
  3. Arun Chandrasekaran, "Why 50% of GenAI Projects Fail - And How to Beat the Odds," Gartner, January 26, 2026 — Gartner reports that at least half of generative AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, names lack of business value as the first failure point, and says projects without a clear way to rank use cases or measure success lack measurable value. The article says Gartner analyzed hundreds of implementations and shows no method.
  4. James Ryseff, Brandon F. De Bruhl, and Sydne J. Newberry, "The Root Causes of Failure for Artificial Intelligence Projects and How They Can Succeed: Avoiding the Anti-Patterns of AI," RAND Corporation, 2024 — RAND interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers, who most often named misunderstanding or miscommunication about a project's purpose as the reason AI projects failed. Most interviewees were engineers, so the results may be skewed toward identifying leadership failures. These are interviews, not a count of projects.
  5. National Institute of Standards and Technology, "AI Risk Management Framework Core," excerpt from AI RMF 1.0, 2023 — NIST's framework asks organizations to define who is responsible when people and AI work together and who oversees the AI, and to document how human oversight works. It is voluntary and does not prescribe this paper's brief.
  6. Johannes-Tobias Lorenz, Joshan Cherian Abraham, Robert Levin, and Douglas Ziman, "From promise to impact: How companies can measure—and realize—the full value of AI," McKinsey & Company, April 24, 2026 — McKinsey separates five levels of measurement for AI value: technical performance, user adoption, operating results, strategic outcomes, and financial impact. It is a consultancy's approach, not a tested standard.
