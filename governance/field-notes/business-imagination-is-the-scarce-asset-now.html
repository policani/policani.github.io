Business imagination is the scarce asset now

AI has made execution cheaper. It has not made value automatic. The next governance question is which work should change, what evidence would prove it, and which human, AI, data, workflow, governance, and funding assets belong on the job.

The argument in one line

Cheap execution is table stakes; advantage moves to the operating imagination that discovers better work, proves it, and assigns the right assets to it.

Execution got cheap. Better questions did not.

AI can now draft, summarize, classify, compare, code, and synthesize at a speed and cost that would have looked unrealistic a few years ago. That is real progress. It also creates a management trap: when execution gets cheap, organizations can produce more work without becoming more selective about which work should exist.

The market signal is consistent. McKinsey's 2025 global AI survey found that 88 percent of respondents report regular AI use in at least one business function, while only 39 percent report enterprise-level EBIT impact from AI use.1 McKinsey's 2026 agentic-organization discussion sharpened the same pattern: more than 80 percent of companies said they were not yet seeing bottom-line impact from their AI investments.2

The gap is not only model capability. Most organizations are still asking AI to accelerate familiar requests: summarize this meeting, draft this note, classify this backlog, write this code pattern, produce this report. Some of that work is useful. It is rarely enough to create advantage.

The scarce capability is business imagination: the operating ability to notice that a workflow, decision, control, product experience, or customer interaction has become possible to redesign; test whether that possibility matters; compare it with other demands for scarce capacity; and assign the right human, AI, data, workflow, governance, and funding assets to it.

Business imagination is not brainstorming.

In this context, imagination is not a personality trait or a permission slip for novelty. It is disciplined option creation from domain context. The useful question is not, "Can AI do this task?" Increasingly, it can. The better question is, "Which business problem is worth redesigning, what value would change if the work changed, and what evidence would prove it?"

That question belongs in portfolio governance. It should show up in an idea register, a business-value register, funding gates, readiness reviews, and value-realization ledgers. An AI idea should name the affected workflow, the decision or outcome it should improve, the current baseline, the value mechanism, the evidence required, the reliance boundary, the owner, and the review cadence.

Without that structure, AI ideas arrive as anecdotes: a vendor demo, a useful prompt, a team experiment, a hallway suggestion, a dashboard no one owns, or an agent prototype that starts shaping decisions before anyone has reviewed its limits. The register turns those fragments into comparable decision inventory.

ROI starts before the pilot.

Most AI ROI conversations start too late. By the time a team is trying to prove value after a pilot, the important choices may already have been made badly: the use case was selected because it was visible, the workflow was never redesigned, the output was not tied to a decision, adoption was assumed, risk controls were added afterward, and total cost was incomplete.

ROI should start as a value hypothesis. The hypothesis does not need to be financially precise on day one, but it must be testable. It should name the workflow, the pain or opportunity, the expected mechanism of value, the human owner, the evidence required to move from exploration to reliance, and the operating assets needed to test the idea.

That makes ROI a chain, not a single number. Technical performance asks whether the system works. Adoption asks whether the intended users actually put it into the workflow. Operational indicators test whether the work changed: less rework, faster cycle time, fewer defects, broader coverage, better exception handling. Strategic outcomes connect that change to the business. Financial impact comes last, after cost, risk, and attribution are understood.3

Asset fit matters more than AI enthusiasm.

"AI resources" is too narrow a phrase. The real resource pool includes human expertise, models, data, workflow artifacts, deterministic automation, governance capacity, funding, and time. The portfolio question is which asset belongs on which job.

Some ideas deserve frontier-model exploration because the option value is high and uncertainty is real. Some should move quickly to cheaper models because the task is known and repeatable. Some need a deterministic script, better source data, a vendor feature, a training path, a human expert, or a policy decision before any AI work is funded. Some should wait.

That routing decision is where portfolio discipline earns its keep. It prevents every AI-flavored request from consuming the same scarce reviewers, engineers, licenses, executive attention, and change capacity.

The operating standard

Business imagination becomes valuable when it is made accountable without being smothered. A useful AI opportunity system has a light intake path for new ideas, a value and risk screen, an asset-assignment decision, a proof path for pilots and scaling, and a value-realization ledger after approval.

The standard is simple: AI opportunities should compete on value logic, evidence readiness, workflow fit, risk control, asset fit, capacity realism, and measurable follow-through, never on novelty.

The leaders who win with AI will not be the ones who ask models to do the old work slightly faster. They will be the ones who build an operating system for discovering better work, proving it, assigning assets to it, and stopping what does not earn the next increment of trust.
