Can Your Portfolio Review Explain Its Funding Decisions?

TL;DR

Accurate status cannot explain a funding choice. Compare the alternatives, check who can act, and record why the review chose.

What the paper develops

An accurate report leaves a funding question open

Picture a portfolio review, the meeting where leaders decide where project money and people go. Every status report is accurate. A request is approved, but nobody can point to a comparison explaining why it beat the alternatives. The decision happened; its basis is hard to inspect.

Status shows how a project is doing against its plan. A funding request asks whether more money and people belong there rather than elsewhere. That question applies to new proposals and running projects asking for their next resources.

Find who can make the choice

The Project Management Institute's portfolio standard gives the governing body power over resources and priorities [1]. It uses status alongside other criteria. First establish who can change the allocation. A review that only recommends should send that decision maker alternatives already compared and record the eventual choice.

Make the comparison visible

PMI groups work by purpose and uses common criteria within each group [1]. Compare strategy, benefit, cost, risk, and dependencies. Dependencies are other work a request needs to succeed. Include the people required, reflecting the Association for Project Management's emphasis on the ability to deliver [2]. Use the organization's agreed scores and rankings to support the choice; record the reason for following or departing from them.

The sponsor, the executive who champions the request, supplies the proposal. The portfolio manager prepares information for evaluation under the standard [1]. Where an office supports the review, have it check the evidence with the sponsor. Match periods and benefit definitions. Show estimate sources and assumptions. Complete evidence is not automatically credible evidence.

Name the alternative even when one request arrives

Compare a lone request with the use of money or people it would actually displace, if any. Also compare leaving available resources uncommitted. This is my recommendation. If it needs people already assigned to a high-priority project, that project is the relevant alternative. A low-ranked project elsewhere may have no staff to release.

Show missing evidence and exceptions

For a request seeking a business benefit, I recommend a hold when essential evidence is missing. Name the missing fact and a return date. If evidence covers one office, consider a proposal limited to that office. The chair may approve despite a gap; record what remains unknown and why proceeding is justified.

Required work means a duty imposed by law, contract, or safety. Compare adequate ways to meet it, showing cost, people, and deadline. An experiment funds learning before a larger decision: compare how the answer could help that decision and the resources needed. State the question, resources, and answer date. The office proposes the category. The review confirms it. These are recommendations, not rules every review must use.

Check the reason rather than count changes

For each request, record what it asked for, what it received, its alternatives, and the reason. Ask whether the review could have changed the allocation and whether the written reason compares those alternatives. A careful decision may leave funding unchanged. A count of stops cannot tell it from an unexamined approval.

This recommendation rests on PMI's third edition and APM's public guidance. It does not prove that the comparison process improves returns. Begin with a recent request, then use the comparison at the next decision. The sponsor supplies evidence, the office prepares it, and the executive explains the choice.

What to do next

Take a recent request and find who could change its allocation, the alternatives compared, and the written reason for the choice.
